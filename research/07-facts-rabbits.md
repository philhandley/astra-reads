## Changes and conflicts with the brief

- **Dawn and dusk.** PDSA says rabbits are "crepuscular", "most active at twilight", and "far more common to see them active during the early morning or evening hours" [checked]. The Mammal Society says rabbits are "normally nocturnal but will sometimes come out in daylight" [checked]. PTES says they are "often active during the day" [checked]. The story can say "most often out at dawn and dusk", but not "only".
- **Mother's visits.** The brief says once or twice a day. The RSPCA page says "Their mother will come back each day to feed them" [checked]. No checked page gives "once or twice", so use the RSPCA wording.
- **Litters.** The Wildlife Trust says one litter "every month during the breeding season" [checked]. PTES says a new litter "every 5 to 6 weeks from February to August" [checked]. Mammal Society says "several litters a year" [checked]. Say "many litters in a year" to avoid the clash.
- **Mostly grass.** The sources list grass first, but none says "mostly". The wording below is my summary.
- **Entrances per warren.** I found no checked source with a number of entrances. See Gaps.
- **Eyes.** PDSA says "nearly 360 degrees" [checked]. A PetsRadar snippet mentions a small blind spot in front and behind [search]. I did not open that page, so keep to "almost all the way round".
- **Norman date.** Mammal Society says the Romans kept rabbits in the 1st century, and "it is most likely the Normans in the 12th century who were responsible for rabbits becoming established" [checked]. The Fishbourne bone is 1st century and was probably kept by people [checked]. Give the Normans as the main story.
- **Pet pages.** PDSA's "10 things" page is about pet rabbits as well as wild ones. Use it only for teeth, eyes and kittens.

## 1. European rabbit basics

- Rabbits are about 30 to 40 cm long, from nose to the start of the tail. Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked] (my comparison: about one school ruler, a bit more).
- An adult rabbit weighs about 1.2 to 2 kg. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked]. The Wildlife Trust gives the same range: https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/wildlife-explorer/mammals/rabbit [checked] (my comparison: one or two bags of sugar from the shop, each about 1 kg).
- Males are usually heavier than females. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked].
- Rabbits are plant eaters. Grasses come first on the list, then cereal crops, root vegetables and young shoots. Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked] (my summary: "mostly grass").
- In winter they eat tree bark. Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked].
- Rabbits are most active at dawn and dusk. Source: https://www.pdsa.org.uk/what-we-do/blog/vet-qas-are-rabbits-nocturnal [checked]. This page says all rabbits, wild and pet, share this pattern. Mammal Society and PTES disagree (see Changes).
- Rabbits' ears are "about the same length as their head". Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked] (my comparison: as long as the rabbit's own head).

## 2. Warrens

- Rabbits live in big groups in underground burrow systems called warrens. Source: https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/wildlife-explorer/mammals/rabbit [checked].
- A warren is "a series of interconnected underground tunnels, dens, and bolt holes". Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked] (my comparison: a house with many rooms, all underground).
- How deep a warren goes "depends on the nature of the soil and the height of the water table". No number is given. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked].
- The group in one warren can be a single pair, or up to 30 rabbits. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked].
- A usual group is "two to eight adults", with separate pecking orders for males and for females. Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked].
- Rabbits rarely go more than 200 metres from their burrows. Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked] (my comparison: about two football pitches, end to end).
- Some burrows have one entrance and are used as breeding "stops". Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked].

## 3. Babies

- Baby rabbits are called kits or kittens. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked] ("Kit: A young rabbit"). PDSA also says "Baby rabbits are called kittens" [checked].
- The mother makes a nest inside a burrow from grass, and "lines it with soft fur from her chest and belly". Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked].
- A litter is 3 to 7 young. Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked]. Mammal Society gives the same range [checked] (my comparison: fewer than the fingers on two hands).
- Kittens are "born blind, deaf and almost hairless". Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked]. Mammal Society says the same [checked].
- Their eyes open at about 10 days, and they first come to the burrow entrance at about 18 days. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked].
- Baby rabbits stay in the burrow until they are about three weeks old. Source: https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/wildlife/rabbits [checked].
- The mother comes back each day to feed them. Source: https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/wildlife/rabbits [checked].
- If you find a baby rabbit or hare alone, "they might not have been abandoned". Source: https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/wildlife/rabbits [checked].
- The RSPCA says the best thing to do is "to leave the animal alone". If it is in danger, move it a short way to safety, then move about 100 metres away and watch from a distance. Source: https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/wildlife/rabbits [checked] (my comparison: about one football pitch).
- Get help if the baby is injured, has been caught by a dog or cat, has closed eyes or no fur, or its mother is dead nearby. Source: https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/wildlife/rabbits [checked].
- If the baby is still there two hours after sunset and the mother has not come back, it needs rescuing. Source: https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/wildlife/rabbits [checked].

## 4. Warning signals

- A rabbit warns the others of danger by thumping its back legs on the ground. Source: https://www.horniman.ac.uk/object/HC.1999.1926/json [checked] (a UK museum record).
- If a rabbit raises its white tail, "all the other rabbits will move quickly back to their warrens". Source: https://www.horniman.ac.uk/object/HC.1999.1926/json [checked] (my comparison: a white flag going up as a warning).
- Rabbits "rely on their long ears to warn them of danger". Source: https://ptes.org/get-informed/facts-figures/rabbit/ [checked].

## 5. Teeth and eyes

- A rabbit's teeth "never stop growing", so chewing is needed to wear them down. Source: https://www.pdsa.org.uk/what-we-do/blog/10-things-you-might-not-know-about-rabbits [checked] (my comparison: like a pencil that never gets shorter unless it is worn down).
- Their eyes are on the sides of the head, so they can see "nearly 360 degrees". Source: https://www.pdsa.org.uk/what-we-do/blog/10-things-you-might-not-know-about-rabbits [checked] (my comparison: almost like having eyes at the back).

## 6. Hares versus rabbits

- Hares are bigger. An adult hare is about 52 to 59 cm long. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/brown-hare [checked] (my comparison: about two school rulers end to end).
- A hare weighs about 3 to 4 kg, about twice as much as a rabbit. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/brown-hare [checked] (my comparison: about three bags of sugar).
- Hares have "very long black-tipped ears" and "large, long, powerful hind legs". Rabbit ears are shorter and have no black tips. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/brown-hare [checked].
- Hares do not use burrows. They make "a small depression in the ground among long grass", called a form. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/brown-hare [checked].
- Baby hares are called leverets. They are "born fully furred with their eyes open". Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/brown-hare [checked].
- A mother hare feeds her leverets "once a day for the first four weeks", at sunset. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/brown-hare [checked]. The RSPCA says the mother "will usually come back around dusk to feed them" [checked].
- Hares have amber eyes, and rabbits have brown eyes. Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/brown-hare [checked].

## 7. Rabbits and farmers

- Rabbits can damage crops. The gov.uk fencing grant is for a site with "evidence of damage caused by rabbits, such as significant burrowing or crop damage". Source: https://www.gov.uk/countryside-stewardship-grants/rabbit-fencing-supplement-fg4 [checked].
- A rabbit-proof fence uses galvanised 18-gauge, 31 mm hexagonal wire mesh. Its bottom edge is buried at least 150 mm, or laid flat on the ground. Source: https://www.gov.uk/countryside-stewardship-grants/rabbit-fencing-supplement-fg4 [checked] (my comparison: the buried edge is a bit shorter than a pencil).
- Some farms leave wild strips at the edges of fields. The gov.uk plan for the Tees Lowlands includes "sowing nectar flower mixes" and "winter bird food mixes", and says field margins can "improve water quality" and "create wildlife habitats and corridors". Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/countryside-stewardship-statement-of-priorities-tees-lowlands-nca023/countryside-stewardship-statement-of-priorities-tees-lowlands-nca023 [checked]. This page is from 21 May 2021 and covers one area only.
- The same plan names brown hares among the farm wildlife it helps. Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/countryside-stewardship-statement-of-priorities-tees-lowlands-nca023/countryside-stewardship-statement-of-priorities-tees-lowlands-nca023 [checked].

## 8. Rabbits came to Britain

- The Romans brought rabbits to Britain in the 1st century, "when they were kept as livestock for food". Source: https://mammal.org.uk/british-mammals/european-rabbit [checked].
- The Normans introduced rabbits "in the 12th century to provide food and fur". Source: https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/wildlife-explorer/mammals/rabbit [checked] (my comparison: about 900 years ago).
- A small rabbit leg bone found at Fishbourne Roman Palace in Sussex is dated to the 1st century AD. Source: https://news-archive.exeter.ac.uk/2019/april/title_712701_en.html [checked]. Researchers think it was kept by people, not living wild.
