## Changes and conflicts with the brief

- **Stoop speed.** Sources agree on about 200 mph (320 km/h) in a dive. The 242 mph (389 km/h) figure is a trained falcon in 1999. Guinness calls the peregrine the fastest bird in a dive. I found no primary source that says "fastest animal", so the story should say "fastest bird". The RSPB page I opened gives no speed.
- **RSPB gaps.** The RSPB page gives no size, egg count or chick details. Those come from the Hawk and Owl Trust and National Geographic Kids.
- **Krill.** The brief says NOAA gives up to about 4 tonnes in summer. The NOAA page says "up to 6 tons of krill a day" for the biggest whales, with no season. Use the NOAA figure.
- **Heart.** The brief says the Royal Ontario Museum (ROM) preserved a heart in 2015. The ROM tracker, seen only in a search snippet, dates removal to May 2014. The "size of a small car" claim is not supported: National Geographic says the heart is "not the size of a car". Use "almost as big as a small piano" instead. The Natural History Museum (NHM) page was not opened.
- **Heart weight.** ROM tracker about 180 kg [search]. Guinness 440 lb (199.5 kg) [checked]. National Geographic about 400 lb [checked]. Use "about 400 pounds (about 180 to 200 kg)".
- **Blue whale size.** Sources give up to 136 tonnes (NOAA PMEL), more than 330,000 lb (NOAA Fisheries), about 160 tons (NPS) and up to 173 tonnes (Countryfile). Use "up to about 150 tonnes". Length: up to about 30 m (Countryfile). NOAA Fisheries says up to about 110 feet (33.5 m) for Antarctic animals.
- **Calf.** Birth weight is 2,700 to 3,600 kg (SeaWorld; PMEL gives up to 3.6 tonnes), but NPS says 5,000 to 6,000 lb. Daily gain: SeaWorld 90 kg (200 lb); NPS says over 250 lb. Use the SeaWorld figures.
- **NOAA gaps.** The NOAA Fisheries blue whale page does not give the spout height or calf figures. Those come from the National Park Service (NPS) and SeaWorld pages.
- **Blue whale population.** The IUCN page returned 403. A search snippet gives about 10,000 to 25,000 (SeaWorld). A New York State plan gives 5,000 to 15,000 mature animals. Use "about 10,000 to 25,000, and the exact number is not known".
- **Peregrine eggs.** Hawk and Owl Trust 3 to 4. National Geographic Kids 2 to 4. London sources (search) 2 to 5. Use "usually 3 or 4".
- **UK pairs.** The BTO page gives 1,769 pairs in 2014 in its text, but 1,750 in its key-stats box. Use 1,769 and give the year. A search snippet attributes "at least 1,500" to the RSPB. A newer survey may exist.
- **DDT.** The RSPB and BTO pages say "pesticides" (BTO: organochlorine pesticides). I did not open a page that names DDT, so use "pesticides".
- **NOAA neutral gear.** The NOAA pages I opened say to stop and slowly move away within 100 yards. The "put the engine in neutral" wording comes from the UK WiSe guidance, not NOAA.
- **Distance.** NOAA's 100 yards is about 91 metres. UK guidance uses 100 metres, so this brief uses 100 metres for UK readers.
- **Webcams.** The cathedral webcam notes are from a spring 2026 list. Cameras may be off in October. Check before planning to watch.

## A. Peregrine falcon ("the fastest animal")

### Speed in the stoop (the dive)

- A peregrine can dive up to 200 miles (323 km) an hour to catch its prey in the air. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/birds/facts/peregrine-falcon [checked] (my comparison: about three times faster than a car on a motorway, where the limit is 70 mph).
- In ideal conditions a stoop reaches about 320 km/h (200 mph). Source: https://guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/70929-fastest-bird-diving [checked].
- A trained falcon called Frightful was reportedly clocked at 242 mph (389 km/h) while stooping. Source: same Guinness page [checked] (my comparison: a trained bird, not a wild one hunting).
- The Hertfordshire Wildlife Trust calls the stoop the fastest recorded animal movement. Source: https://www.hertswildlifetrust.org.uk/sites/default/files/2023-05/Peregrine%20Falcon%20Fact%20File%202023_0.pdf [search].

### Size

- The average length is 42 cm (my comparison: a little longer than a ruler). Source: https://www.hawkandowltrust.org/learn-and-discover/about-birds-of-prey/peregrine [checked].
- The wingspan is about 102 cm, about one metre across (my comparison: about as wide as a dining chair is tall). Same source [checked].
- Females weigh about 1.1 kg and males about 670 g (my comparison: a female is about as heavy as a bag of sugar). Same source [checked].
- The body is 14 to 19 inches long and the wingspan is 3.3 to 3.6 feet. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/birds/facts/peregrine-falcon [checked].

### Food

- They mostly catch medium-sized birds, such as wading birds and pigeons. Source: https://www.rspb.org.uk/birds-and-wildlife/peregrine-falcon [checked].
- Starlings, pigeons and doves are among their favourite meals. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/birds/facts/peregrine-falcon [checked].

### Where they nest

- Traditionally they nest on sea cliffs, rocky crags and quarries. They now also use tall buildings, such as cathedrals, in towns. Source: https://www.rspb.org.uk/birds-and-wildlife/peregrine-falcon [checked].
- Derby Cathedral has two webcams on the nest, at the side of the tower. Source: https://derby.gov.uk/environment-and-planning/conservation/peregrine-falcons-in-derby/webcams [checked]. The page says the birds usually fledge (first fly from the nest) in mid to late June [checked].
- Norwich Cathedral's webcam was live in March 2026, and an egg was laid on 24 March 2026. Source: https://englishcathedrals.co.uk/latest-news/updated-cathedral-peregrine-webcams/ [checked].
- Chichester Cathedral's webcam is live (2026 list). Same source [checked].
- Worcester Cathedral's webcam was live from 26 February 2026. Same source [checked].
- Salisbury Cathedral's webcam is live (2026 list). Same source [checked].

### Eggs and chicks

- The female lays 3 or 4 eggs. Source: https://www.hawkandowltrust.org/learn-and-discover/about-birds-of-prey/peregrine [checked]. National Geographic Kids says 2 to 4 [checked].
- The first chicks are white and fluffy (my comparison: like a soft toy). Source: https://community.rspb.org.uk/placestovisit/dovestone/b/dovestone-blog/posts/peregrine-chicks [search].
- The chicks stay in the nest for up to six weeks, by which time they have learned to fly. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/birds/facts/peregrine-falcon [checked].
- The Hawk and Owl Trust gives 39 to 40 days until fledging (my comparison: about one and a half months). Source: https://www.hawkandowltrust.org/learn-and-discover/about-birds-of-prey/peregrine [checked].

### Numbers

- In the 1960s peregrines were at a low point, because of human persecution and pesticides in the food chain. Source: https://www.rspb.org.uk/birds-and-wildlife/peregrine-falcon [checked].
- In 1961 there were only 385 pairs in the UK (my comparison: about the number of pupils in a big primary school). Source: https://bto.org/about-birds/bird-of-month/peregrine [checked].
- Peregrine numbers have now recovered. Source: https://www.rspb.org.uk/birds-and-wildlife/peregrine-falcon [checked].
- The latest full UK count I found is 1,769 pairs in 2014, up 22% since 2002. Source: https://bto.org/about-birds/bird-of-month/peregrine [checked].

### The law

- Peregrines are on Schedule 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1981/69/schedule/1 [checked].
- It is an offence to disturb one while it is building a nest, or is in, on or near a nest with eggs or young. It is also an offence to disturb its young. Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1981/69/section/1 [checked] (England and Wales).
- The word "recklessly" was added in 2000 by the Countryside and Rights of Way Act. Same source [checked].
- It is also an offence to take, damage or destroy a nest that is in use. Same source [checked].

### Watch points and volunteers

- At Lincoln Cathedral in 2016, 25 volunteers from the RSPB's Lincoln local group ran telescopes for visitors on Saturdays and Sundays in June and July. Source: https://lincolncathedral.com/latest-news/another-great-year-peregrines-lincoln-cathedral/ [checked] (a 2016 article, so check this year's dates).
- At Salisbury Cathedral, RSPB Date with Nature volunteers set up telescopes outside for four weeks from 10 June, six days a week except Sundays. Source: https://www.salisburycathedral.org.uk/arrangements/a-date-with-nature/ [search] (the year is not in the snippet).

## B. Blue whale and whale watching

### Biggest animal

- The blue whale is the largest animal that has ever lived. Source: https://www.countryfile.com/go-outdoors/days-out/blue-whale-arrives-at-natural-history-museum [checked] (a news article about the NHM display; the museum page was not opened).
- The NHM's blue whale skeleton is about 25 metres long and weighs about 3 tonnes. Same source [checked] (my comparison: about two and a half double-decker buses, since a bus is about 10 m long).

### Size and weight

- Blue whales can be up to about 30 metres long. Source: https://www.countryfile.com/go-outdoors/days-out/blue-whale-arrives-at-natural-history-museum [checked] (my comparison: about three double-decker buses end to end).
- They average 26 m long and weigh up to 136 metric tonnes. Source: https://pmel.noaa.gov/acoustics/whales/whale-biology-blue.html [checked] (my comparison: roughly 20 African elephants).
- Antarctic blue whales can reach about 110 feet (33.5 m) and weigh more than 330,000 pounds. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/blue-whale [checked].

### Food

- Blue whales feed on krill, which gather in dense patches in the sea. Source: https://pmel.noaa.gov/acoustics/whales/whale-biology-blue.html [checked] (my comparison: krill are tiny, a bit like shrimp).
- Some of the biggest blue whales may eat up to 6 tons of krill in a day. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/blue-whale [checked] (my comparison: about as heavy as five or six small cars).

### Calls

- Blue whales are among the loudest animals on the planet. Their sounds are pulses, groans and moans. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/blue-whale [checked].
- Their sounds can be heard by other whales up to 1,000 miles away. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/blue-whale [checked] (my comparison: a bit further than from London to Rome).

### Calf

- A newborn calf is about 7 metres long (about 23 feet). Source: https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/baleen-whales/care-of-young/ [checked]. PMEL gives 7 to 8 m [checked] (my comparison: a little shorter than a double-decker bus).
- A newborn calf weighs about 2,700 to 3,600 kg (my comparison: about three small cars). Source: https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/baleen-whales/care-of-young/ [checked].
- A calf gains about 90 kg (200 lb) each day while nursing (my comparison: about as heavy as a grown-up, every day). Source: https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/baleen-whales/care-of-young/ [checked]. NPS says "over 250 pounds a day" [checked] (https://www.nps.gov/places/blue-whale.htm).

### Spout

- The spout is made of air and water and rises about 30 feet (about 9 metres). Source: https://www.nps.gov/places/blue-whale.htm [checked] (my comparison: about as high as a three-floor house).

### Heart

- A blue whale's heart is about 5 feet long, 4 feet wide and 5 feet tall, and it weighs about 400 pounds. Source: https://blog.education.nationalgeographic.org/2015/08/31/how-big-is-a-blue-whales-heart/ [checked].
- It is "almost as big as a small piano" and weighs about the same as a filled oil drum. Same source [checked]. It is not the size of a car.
- Guinness World Records gives a heart record of 440 pounds (199.5 kg), about 5 feet (1.5 m) from top to bottom. Source: https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/largest-heart [checked]. Guinness says this is "significantly smaller" than the Volkswagen Beetle claim. Same source [checked].
- The Royal Ontario Museum in Toronto has a preserved blue whale heart. Its tracker lists it at 180 kg (about 397 lb). Source: https://rom.on.ca/en/node/16897 [search] (the page returned 404; the figures come from a search snippet).

### Endangered and numbers

- Blue whales are listed as endangered under the US Endangered Species Act. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/blue-whale [checked].
- The global IUCN category is endangered (Cooke 2018). Source: https://oap.ospar.org/en/versions/1117-en-1-0-1-blue-whale/ [checked] (an OSPAR summary; the IUCN page returned 403).
- About 10,000 to 25,000 blue whales are estimated in total. Source: https://seaworld.org/animals/facts/mammals/blue-whale/ [search]. A New York State plan gives 5,000 to 15,000 mature animals and says the global number is unknown. Source: https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/fs/programs/dfw/SWAP2025/Mammals-Marine/Blue%20whale%20SSA.pdf [search].
- Iceland had about 3,000 blue whales in 2015. Source: https://oap.ospar.org/en/versions/1117-en-1-0-1-blue-whale/ [checked] (Iceland only).
- Some populations are rising locally, but recovery is slow. Same source [checked].

### Where to see them

- Monterey Bay, California: blue whales are in the sanctuary from late spring to late autumn. Source: https://monterey.gov/your_city_hall/departments/public_works/harbor_and_marina/national_marine_sanctuary.php [checked].
- Mirissa, on Sri Lanka's south coast, is a common base for blue whale trips. Source: https://www.responsibletravel.com/holidays/sri-lanka/travel-guide/blue-whale-watching-in-sri-lanka [search] (a travel guide; check current operators).

### Whale-watching rules

UK (the WiSe scheme, which the Marine Management Organisation recommends to boat operators):

- Stay 100 metres away from whales and dolphins. Source: https://plymouthport.org.uk/reminder-of-rules-for-protected-marine-mammals/ [checked] (repeats the MMO guidance).
- Avoid groups with mothers and young completely. Same source [checked].
- If whales come over to the boat, switch the engine to neutral. Same source [checked].
- Do not touch them, because that could be an offence. Same source [checked].
- In 2014 two boat skippers were prosecuted for reckless disturbance of a pod of dolphins. Same source [checked].
- Disturbing whales or dolphins intentionally or recklessly is an offence, with up to six months in prison and/or an unlimited fine. Source: https://gov.uk/government/news/boat-operators-reminded-of-rules-on-protected-marine-mammals [search].

UK code (Thanet Coast Project, for recreational boats):

- Keep slow, with no wake, and do not chase or harass wildlife. Source: https://www.thanetcoast.org.uk/thanet-coastal-codes/marine-wildlife-watching-code/ [checked].
- Do not head into the group, because that scatters and separates them. Same source [checked].
- Spend no more than 15 minutes near the animals, and keep 200 m away when two boats are present. Same source [checked].

US (NOAA):

- Observe whales from at least 100 yards (about 91 metres) away. NOAA generally advises not to come closer than 300 feet, the length of a football field. Source: https://farallones.noaa.gov/eco/whales/responsible-recreation-viewing-guidelines.html [checked].
- If a whale is within 100 yards, stop immediately and slowly move away. Same source [checked].
