# Astra Reads: real facts for four stories (04)

For a reader aged about six. Four sections: A. Maiasaura, B. Mars base and black holes, C. Mountain gorillas, D. The ocean.

## How to read the tags

- **[search]** The fact came from a search result snippet. The page was blocked (403) or not opened.
- **[check]** Sources disagree, or the figure is from a fan wiki or news report. Confirm before printing.

## Changes and conflicts with the brief

- **Maiasaura age.** The brief says about 76 million years ago. The London Natural History Museum (NHM) says 80 to 75 million. The National Park Service (NPS) says about 77 million. The brief's 76 sits between them.
- **Who found Egg Mountain.** The Carleton SERC page says a rock-shop family (Marion Brandvold) found the bones in spring 1978, and Horner and Makela identified them that summer. The Old Trail Museum credits Laurie Trexler of Bynum with the 1978 discovery. For the story, say a rock-shop family found the bones.
- **Gorilla food.** The brief says up to 18 kg a day. IFAW says up to 34 kg (75 lb) of plant food a day. Use 34 kg.
- **Gorilla population.** The brief says about 1,000. Mongabay gives at least 1,063 (604 in the Virungas in 2016, 459 in Bwindi-Sarambwe in 2018). IFAW quotes an IUCN figure of about 600 mature gorillas. Use "about 1,000" and say so.
- **Gorilla distance.** Research supports 7 metres (berggorilla.org). Some 2026 travel blogs and an allAfrica report say 10 metres, but I found no official Uganda Wildlife Authority notice. Use 7 metres and check before the trip. [search: https://allafrica.com/stories/202607200709.html]
- **Olympus Mons height.** NASA's volcano page says 27 km. NASA's Mars facts page says "40 kilometers" from base to summit. Use 27 km.
- **Chimp DNA.** NHGRI says 96 per cent of the DNA sequence matches. The American Museum of Natural History says 98.8 per cent. The methods differ, so say which one you use.
- **Green turtle size.** NOAA says 3 to 4 feet and 250 to 400 lb. National Geographic Kids says up to 5 feet and 700 lb. Use NOAA.
- **Octopus suckers.** The Seattle Aquarium says about 200 per arm. Another site says about 280. Use 200.
- **Mars day.** The brief's 24 h 37 min is the star-based (sidereal) day. NASA's solar day is 24.66 hours, about 24 h 40 min. Use 40 minutes for "a day".
- **Pavonis Mons pit.** The 35 m skylight is one feature. HiRISE image ESP_019351_1795 shows a different 180 m pit on the south-east slope. Do not mix them.

## A. Maiasaura ("good mother lizard")

### When and where

- Maiasaura lived in the Late Cretaceous, about 76 to 77 million years ago. Source: https://nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/maiasaura.html [checked] (NHM says 80 to 75 million). Source: https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked] (NPS says about 77 million).
- Its fossils were found at Egg Mountain, just south of Choteau, Montana. Source: https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked]
- Its bones come from the Two Medicine Formation, a rock layer in Montana. Source: https://www.britannica.com/print/article/358406 [search] (Britannica page blocked when fetched.)
- In 1978 a rock-shop family near Choteau found some fossil bones. Source: https://serc.carleton.edu/research_education/mt_geoheritage/sites/augusta_choteau/paleontology.html [checked]
- Jack Horner and Bob Makela studied the bones that summer and found they came from a baby duck-billed dinosaur. Source: same SERC page [checked]. (Picture: a duck-bill, like a duck's beak on a dinosaur's face.)
- The Old Trail Museum credits Laurie Trexler, of Bynum, with the 1978 discovery. Source: https://oldtrailmuseum.org/egg-mountain/ [checked] (conflicts with SERC; see above).
- Horner and Makela named the species in 1979. Source: https://si.edu/object/nmnhpaleobiology_3440766 [search] (Smithsonian record title in search result; page blocked.)
- The name means "good mother reptile". Source: https://oldtrailmuseum.org/egg-mountain/ [checked]

### Nests, eggs and babies

- Horner's team found 14 dinosaur nests in one area of the site. Source: https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked]
- The nests were about 23 feet (about 7 metres) apart, on average. Source: https://serc.carleton.edu/research_education/mt_geoheritage/sites/augusta_choteau/paleontology.html [checked] (Picture: about 14 of your big steps.)
- The eggs were about the size of an ostrich egg. Source: https://dinopedia.fandom.com/wiki/Maiasaura [check] (fan wiki; no museum source found.)
- Each nest held about 30 to 40 eggs. Source: https://dinopedia.fandom.com/wiki/Maiasaura [check] (fan wiki.)
- A baby Maiasaura weighed less than 1 kg (2.2 lb) when it hatched. Source: https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked] (Picture: about as heavy as a bag of sugar.)
- A newly hatched baby was about 0.5 m long. Source: https://www.britannica.com/print/article/358406 [search] (Picture: about as long as a big dog.) Confirm: this is the only source found.
- Babies hatched "big-eyed" and "wobbly-legged". Source: https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/laelaps/baby-dinosaurs-were-born-into-a-world-of-danger [checked]

### Growing up, food and herds

- An adult was about 9 metres long. Source: https://nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/maiasaura.html [checked] (Picture: as long as a double-decker bus.)
- An adult weighed about 2,500 kg (NHM) or about 2,000 kg (NPS). Source: https://nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/maiasaura.html [checked]; https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked] (Picture: about 30 grown-ups.)
- Maiasaura was a plant-eater. Source: https://nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/maiasaura.html [checked]
- The grown-ups may have cared for their young for a long time. Source: https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked] (NPS: "may have actively cared for their young for a significant amount of time.")
- The fossil teeth of the young are worn, so the parents probably brought them food at the nest. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiasaura [check] (secondary; the wording on the NPS page is weaker.)
- Maiasaura lived in large groups that nested together. Source: https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked] ("colonial nesting behavior, where large groups, likely herds, would all nest together.")
- Maiasaura lived in herds all its life. Source: https://oldtrailmuseum.org/egg-mountain/ [checked]
- The herds moved from place to place to find food. Source: https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesozoic-egg-mountain-dawson-2014.htm [checked] (NPS: "seasonally migrated to meet their food needs.")

### A safe "scary" neighbour (no gore)

- Daspletosaurus horneri was a big tyrannosaur, about 9 metres long and 2.2 metres tall. Source: https://www.sci.news/paleontology/daspletosaurus-horneri-new-tyrannosaur-04751.html [checked] (Picture: as long as the Maiasaura parent.)
- It lived in what is now Montana, 75.2 to 74.4 million years ago. Source: https://www.sci.news/paleontology/daspletosaurus-horneri-new-tyrannosaur-04751.html [checked]
- It was named after Jack Horner. Source: https://www.sci.news/paleontology/daspletosaurus-horneri-new-tyrannosaur-04751.html [checked]
- Daspletosaurus was a meat-eater. Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/daspletosaurus.html [checked] (NHM lists its find as Canada, which is the genus in general, not horneri.)
- Troodon lived in North America about 79 to 75 million years ago and ate plants and meat. Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/troodon.html [checked]
- Troodon skeletons and eggs were found at Egg Mountain. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiasaura [check] (secondary; not confirmed on a museum page.)

## B. Mars base and black holes

### Mars: days, years and travel

- A day on Mars is about 24 hours and 40 minutes. Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html [checked] (solar day 24.66 hours.) (Picture: about 40 minutes longer than ours.)
- A year on Mars is 687 Earth days. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/ [checked] (Picture: almost two of our years.)
- Mars is between about 55 million and 401 million km away from Earth. Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html [checked]
- The Perseverance rover flew for 203 days, about six and a half months, to get to Mars. Source: https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/mars_2020/landing/mission [checked] (Picture: about two school terms.)
- Perseverance launched on 30 July 2020 and landed on 18 February 2021. Source: https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/mars_2020/landing/mission [checked]

### Cold, thin air and dust

- The average temperature on Mars is about -59 °C. Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html [checked] (Picture: much colder than a home freezer, which is about -18 °C. Freezer temperature is my comparison.)
- Temperatures can reach 20 °C and drop to about -153 °C. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/ [checked]
- The air pressure on Mars is less than 1 per cent of Earth's. Mars is 6.36 millibars against Earth's 1,014. Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html [checked]; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html [checked]
- The thin air is mostly carbon dioxide, nitrogen and argon. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/ [checked]
- In 2018 a dust storm grew until it covered a quarter of the planet by 12 June. Source: https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/30983 [checked]
- The dust blocked the sunlight that a solar-powered rover needed to charge its batteries. Source: https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/30983 [checked]

### Shelter, radiation and caves

- Mars has no global magnetic field like Earth's. Source: https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/12046 [checked]
- Without that shield, the solar wind strips gas from the top of the atmosphere. Source: https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/12046 [checked]
- Surface radiation was about half the level measured on the journey to Mars. Source: https://www.csmonitor.com/Science/2013/1209/Curiosity-measures-radiation-at-Martian-surface [search] (2013 news report of the Curiosity RAD results.)
- A hole on the volcano Pavonis Mons is about 35 m wide. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2020-march-1-a-hole-in-mars/ [checked]
- Behind the hole is a cavern roughly 20 m deep. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2020-march-1-a-hole-in-mars/ [checked] (Picture: as deep as a six-storey building.)
- Lava tubes might be big enough to shelter people underground, but this is still a proposal. Source: https://www.iflscience.com/researchers-identify-underground-lava-tubes-on-mars-that-could-shelter-first-explorers-56012 [search]

### Water

- In 2008 the Phoenix lander found water in a soil sample near Mars' north pole. Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/text/phoenix_pr_20080731.txt [checked] (NASA release dated 31 July 2008.)
- An orbiter found bright ice in fresh craters at five sites. Source: https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/news/mars-reconnaissance-orbiter-sees-ice-exposed-by-meteor-impact/ [checked]
- The ice is halfway between the north pole and the equator, under the surface. Source: https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/news/mars-reconnaissance-orbiter-sees-ice-exposed-by-meteor-impact/ [checked]
- The new craters were between half a metre and 2.5 metres deep. Source: https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/news/mars-reconnaissance-orbiter-sees-ice-exposed-by-meteor-impact/ [checked] (Picture: about as deep as a tall grown-up.)

### Mountains and moons

- Olympus Mons is 27 km high and more than 600 km across at its base. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/mars/volcanoes/olympus-mons/ [checked] (NASA facts page says 40 km; see conflicts.)
- Olympus Mons is the largest known volcano in the Solar System. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/mars/volcanoes/olympus-mons/ [checked]
- Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/ [checked]
- Phobos is about 26 km across at its widest, and Deimos about 16 km. Source: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html [checked] (fact-sheet radii of 13.0 km and 7.8 km, doubled.)

### Black holes

- A black hole is so dense that nothing, not even light, can escape its pull. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/ [checked]
- Black holes do not suck in everything nearby. From far away they pull just like any other object with the same mass. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/ [checked]
- The nearest known black hole, Gaia BH1, is about 1,500 light-years away. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/ [checked]
- Sagittarius A* is the black hole at the centre of our galaxy, the Milky Way. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]
- Sagittarius A* is about 27,000 light-years from Earth. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked] (Picture: its light takes 27,000 years to reach us.)
- It is about four million times as heavy as our Sun. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]
- Its first picture was released on 12 May 2022. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]
- The picture shows a bright ring around a dark shadow, because the black hole itself is completely dark. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]
- From Earth, the shadow looks about the size of a doughnut on the Moon. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]
- The telescope recorded its data on several nights in 2017. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]

## C. Mountain gorillas

### Where they live and how many

- Mountain gorillas live in two isolated areas across three countries in East-Central Africa. Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked]
- The two areas are the Virunga Mountains and Bwindi (Bwindi-Sarambwe). Source: https://news.mongabay.com/2019/12/mountain-gorilla-census-reveals-further-increase-in-numbers/ [checked]
- There are about 1,000 mountain gorillas. The 2016 Virunga count was 604, and the 2018 Bwindi-Sarambwe count was 459, a total of at least 1,063. Source: https://news.mongabay.com/2019/12/mountain-gorilla-census-reveals-further-increase-in-numbers/ [checked] (IFAW quotes about 600 mature gorillas; see conflicts.)

### Size, food and nests

- A silverback can weigh up to 180 kg (397 lb). Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked] (Picture: about as heavy as two grown-ups.)
- A silverback can be up to 170 cm tall when on all fours. Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked] (Picture: about as tall as a grown-up.)
- Male mountain gorillas turn silver between 11 and 13 years old. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/mountain-gorilla [checked]
- A female weighs about 90 kg (198 lb). Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked]
- An adult can eat up to 34 kg (75 lb) of plants in one day. Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked] (Picture: about as much as a big bag of potatoes. Potato bag weight is my comparison.)
- They eat a variety of plants, with a few insects and worms. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/mountain-gorilla [checked]
- At night they make a nest to sleep in. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/mountain-gorilla [checked]
- The nests are made from foliage. Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked]
- Lighter gorillas often nest in trees, and heavier ones nest on the ground. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/mountain-gorilla [checked]
- The silverback is often the one who does the chest pounding and charging. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/mountain-gorilla [checked]

### Family groups and the 7-metre rule

- A family group, called a troop or band, has a silverback, a young male or two, a few females and their babies. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/mountain-gorilla [checked]
- Visitors must keep at least 7 metres from the gorillas. Source: https://www.berggorilla.org/en/home/news-archive/article-view/the-7-metre-gorilla-tracking-regulation/ [checked] (Picture: about 14 of your big steps.)
- The rule is there because germs can pass between people and gorillas. Source: https://www.berggorilla.org/en/home/news-archive/article-view/the-7-metre-gorilla-tracking-regulation/ [checked] ("The closer humans are to gorillas the higher are the chances that pathogens can be spread.")

### Baby gorillas

- A baby mountain gorilla weighs about 1.8 kg (4 lb) at birth. Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked] (Picture: about as heavy as a bag of sugar.)
- Babies snuggle with their mothers at night. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/mountain-gorilla [checked]
- A mother spends several years raising each baby. Source: https://www.ifaw.org/international/animals/mountain-gorillas [checked]

### Chimpanzees and orangutans (for later chapters)

- About 96 out of every 100 letters of the DNA sequence are the same in humans and chimpanzees, according to NHGRI. Source: https://www.genome.gov/15515096/2005-release-new-genome-comparison-finds-chimps-humans-very-similar-at-dna-level [search] (AMNH gives 98.8 per cent by a different method.)
- In 1960 Jane Goodall saw a chimpanzee strip leaves from a twig and push it into a termite mound to catch termites. Source: https://history.com/this-day-in-history/jane-goodall-observes-a-chimpanzee-making-and-using-tools [checked] (Gombe Stream National Park, Tanzania.)
- Orangutans are the world's largest tree-living mammals, and they live in the forests of Borneo and Sumatra. Source: https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/news/why-do-orangutans [checked]

## D. The ocean

### Giant Pacific octopus

- A giant Pacific octopus has three hearts, eight arms and blue blood. Source: https://www.seattleaquarium.org/animals/giant-pacific-octopus [checked]
- Two of its hearts pump blood through the gills, and one pumps it around the body. Source: https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-does-an-octopus-have-more-than-one-heart [checked]
- Its blood is blue because it contains a copper-rich protein called haemocyanin. Source: https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-does-an-octopus-have-more-than-one-heart [checked]
- Each arm has about 200 suckers, so about 1,600 in all. Source: https://www.seattleaquarium.org/animals/giant-pacific-octopus [checked] (1,600 is 8 arms times 200; arithmetic.)
- An adult usually weighs 40 to 100 lb (about 18 to 45 kg). Source: https://www.seattleaquarium.org/animals/giant-pacific-octopus [checked] (Picture: about as heavy as a six-year-old.)
- When relaxed, an adult is 12 to 14 feet (about 4 metres) from one arm tip to the other. Source: https://www.seattleaquarium.org/animals/giant-pacific-octopus [checked] (Picture: about as long as a car.)
- The largest ones weigh up to 160 lb and are about 25 feet from arm tip to arm tip. Source: https://www.seattleaquarium.org/animals/giant-pacific-octopus [checked] (Picture: about as long as a minibus.)
- It can change its colour at will to show its mood. Source: https://www.seattleaquarium.org/animals/giant-pacific-octopus [checked]
- It is a den-dweller and likes tight, small spaces where it feels safe. Source: https://www.seattleaquarium.org/animals/giant-pacific-octopus [checked]

### Common octopus

- A common octopus has eight arms. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/common-octopus [checked]
- It can grow to about 4.3 feet (about 1.3 m) and weigh up to 22 lb (about 10 kg), but most are smaller. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/common-octopus [checked]
- It lives in tropical and temperate seas all around the world. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/common-octopus [checked]
- It lives about one to two years in the wild. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/common-octopus [checked]
- National Geographic describes it as one of the most intelligent of all invertebrates (animals without a backbone). Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/common-octopus [checked]

### Green sea turtle

- A green sea turtle is a reptile, so it must come up to the surface to breathe air. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle [checked]
- When resting, it can stay underwater for up to five hours. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/reptiles/green-sea-turtle [checked] (Picture: nearly as long as a school day.)
- When it is active, it stays under for a few minutes and then comes up for a few seconds of breath. Source: https://kids.nationalgeographic.com/animals/reptiles/green-sea-turtle [checked]
- Adults are 3 to 4 feet (about 1 m) long and weigh 250 to 400 lb (about 113 to 181 kg). Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle [checked] (Picture: about as heavy as two grown-ups.)
- It is the largest hard-shelled sea turtle. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle [checked]
- Green turtles nest on beaches in more than 80 countries. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle [checked]
- The biggest nesting place is Tortuguero, in Costa Rica, where about 30,000 females nest each season. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle [checked]
- A female lays about 110 eggs in one nest. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle [checked] (National Geographic Kids says about 115.)
- The eggs hatch after about two months in warm sand. Source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle [checked]

### Scuba divers

- A scuba tank holds air at high pressure, usually about 3,000 pounds per square inch. Source: https://dan.org/alert-diver/article/breathe-in-breathe-out/ [search] (DAN article, summary only.)
- The regulator has three parts: a first stage on the tank, a second stage in the diver's mouth, and a hose linking them. Source: https://dan.org/alert-diver/article/breathe-in-breathe-out/ [search]
- Air only flows when the diver breathes in, which is why a regulator is called "demand-type". Source: https://dan.org/alert-diver/article/breathe-in-breathe-out/ [search]
- Divers use hand signals so they understand each other underwater, which DAN says makes diving safer. Source: https://dan.org/alert-diver/article/whats-that-you-say/ [checked]
- The OK sign is a thumb and finger making a circle, with the other fingers out. Source: https://dan.org/alert-diver/article/whats-that-you-say/ [checked] (DAN says some divers use a closed fist instead.)

### Sunlight, glowing animals and plankton

- Sunlight reaches down about 200 metres (656 ft) into the sea, the top layer called the "sunlit" zone. Source: https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/light_travel.html [checked] (Picture: about two football pitches laid end to end. Pitch length is my comparison.)
- Some light can be detected down to about 1,000 m, but there is rarely much light beyond 200 m. Source: https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/light_travel.html [checked]
- About 76 per cent of ocean animals are bioluminescent, which means they make their own light. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/reference/bioluminescence-animals-ocean-glowing/ [checked]
- They make light through chemical reactions, or they host bacteria that do. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/reference/bioluminescence-animals-ocean-glowing/ [checked]
- Glowing sea animals use light to talk to each other, to find food and to hide. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/reference/bioluminescence-animals-ocean-glowing/ [checked]
- A comb jelly, a close cousin of the jellyfish, shimmers in the dark. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/reference/bioluminescence-animals-ocean-glowing/ [checked]
- Millions of tiny one-celled plankton together make a shimmering light in the sea. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/reference/bioluminescence-animals-ocean-glowing/ [checked]

### Robots that explore the deep sea

- An ROV (remotely operated vehicle) is a robot with no crew inside, controlled by people on a ship. Source: https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/rov.html [checked]
- A cable, called a tether, links the ROV to the ship and carries signals both ways. Source: https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/rov.html [checked]
- Most ROVs have cameras and lights, and some have arms for picking things up. Source: https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/rov.html [checked]
