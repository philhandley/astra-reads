## 1. Blank's Levels of Questioning

### What it is
- Set out by Blank, Rose and Berlin (1978). Four levels, from concrete (what is in front of the child) to abstract (reasoning about what is not there).
- UK speech and language therapists use it to pitch questions at a child's level.
- I could not read the 1978 book. The descriptions below come from a local authority handout and an NHS children's speech and language therapy sheet.
- Level names vary by source. "Selective attention" is Level 2 ("Selective Analysis of Perception"). "Recall" is not a level of its own. Remembering sits under Levels 1 and 2.

### The four levels

| Level | Name in handout | What the child does | Average age (NHS sheet) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Matching Perception | Points to, matches, names or remembers what they saw | 2 to 3 |
| 2 | Selective Analysis of Perception | Describes a scene, picks out a named detail, says what something is for, compares two things | 3 to 4 |
| 3 | Reordering Perception | Puts events in order, retells, says how someone feels, says what might happen next, follows two steps | 4 to 5 |
| 4 | Reasoning about Perception | Explains why, works out causes, predicts from reasons, solves a problem, justifies a choice | 5 and older |

- The NHS sheet gives these as averages. Some 2-year-olds manage Level 2 and 3 questions. Some 4-year-olds still need Level 1 and 2.
- The sheet says children need to handle all four levels to work easily in a Year 1 classroom. Finn, at 6, should manage all four.
- Your examples fit the levels:
  - "What did you see?" is Level 1.
  - "What is happening here?" is Level 2.
  - "What will happen next?" is Level 3. Prediction also appears at Level 4.
  - "Why" and "What would happen if" are Level 4.

### Example stems for a story about two brothers exploring
Ten per level, written for this brief from the handout's question types.

Level 1: matching, naming, remembering what was seen
- What did the brothers see?
- Show me the torch.
- Find the boat in the picture.
- What is this? (point to the rope)
- What colour is the tent?
- Who is holding the map?
- Show me the bridge.
- Which brother has the red hat?
- What did they pack in the bag?
- What did you hear in the wood?

Level 2: describing, recalling a named detail, function, comparison
- What is happening in this picture?
- Where did the brothers go?
- What is the map for?
- What did the older brother carry?
- Tell me two things the brothers saw by the river.
- How are the two brothers different?
- What do we use a torch for?
- Tell me something that lives in the forest.
- What did the younger brother say?
- What colour was the rope?

Level 3: order, feelings, retelling, what happens next, two steps
- What happened after they crossed the stream?
- Put these pictures in order to tell the story.
- How do you think the younger brother felt when the path split?
- What might the older brother say to cheer him up?
- What will happen after they find the box?
- What did they do first, and then what did they do?
- Tell me what happened on the trip.
- What does "explore" mean in this story?
- How are the two maps the same?
- Which one of these did not happen in the story?

Level 4: why, causes, what if, solving problems, justifying
- Why did the brothers stop by the river?
- What would have happened if they had not taken the torch?
- Why did the younger brother want to turn back?
- How can we tell the brothers were brave?
- What might they do if the bridge is broken?
- What made the lamp go out?
- Why was the high path better than the low one?
- Why do you think they left the map behind?
- How could they get across the water?
- Why do you think the cave was so dark?

### Turning a stem into a three-option question
- One correct option and two wrong ones. Put the correct option in a different position each time.
- The correct answer should come from the story, but use different words from the text. Finn then has to understand the answer, not just match words.
- Keep options the same type and length: all pictures, or all short phrases.
- Make wrong options plausible: something that could happen in the story, or a detail from the same picture.
- Avoid giveaways: "all of these", a correct option that is the only one with a colour or detail, or an option that repeats the question word.

What makes a good wrong option, by level
- Level 1: other objects from the same picture, or a close match (torch or lamp). Avoid silly options such as a dinosaur.
- Level 2: true details about the story that do not answer this question (the younger brother had the map, not the older one).
- Level 3: events from the same story in the wrong order, or feelings that fit another moment (scared at the bridge, when the question is about the cave).
- Level 4: reasons that sound sensible but are not in the text (it was raining, when the story says it was sunny). Each wrong reason should be something a child could believe.

Worked examples
- Level 1: "What did the brothers take to light the cave?" A) picnic basket B) torch (correct) C) kite
- Level 2: "Where did the brothers camp?" A) on a mountain top B) by the river (correct) C) in a shop
- Level 3: "What happened right after the bridge broke?" A) They ate their lunch B) They tied a rope to a tree (correct) C) They went home
- Level 4: "Why did the brothers wait for morning?" A) Because they were tired of walking B) Because the path was too dark to see (correct) C) Because it was their birthday

## 2. Little Wandle and comprehension

### How a reading practice session is built
- Each book is read in three sessions, each with one focus. Sessions are 20 minutes, three times a week.
- The guidance recommends using the same book across all three sessions.
- A short warm-up before Read 1 rehearses letter sounds, tricky words and new vocabulary.
- Read 1, decoding: apply phonic knowledge to read the words.
- Read 2, prosody: read aloud with meaning, stress and intonation. The child reads alone first, then the adult works on expression.
- Read 3, comprehension: checks understanding of the book's events, characters and information.
- The Little Wandle pages I could open do not name comprehension question types.

### Question types used in Years 1 and 2
Taken from Year 2 comprehension guidance and school materials. These are not Little Wandle's own question bank.
- Retrieval: find a fact in the text. Stems start with who, what, where, when or how.
- Inference: work out what is not said directly, using words and pictures. Stem: "How do you know?"
- Prediction: what might happen next, based on what has been read so far.
- Vocabulary: what a word means, using nearby words. Stem: "Find a word that means..."
- Sequencing: put events in order. Stem: "What happened first, next, at the end?"
- Explain: say why, pointing to the text. Some schools ask children to give a "because" reason for each answer.

Rough match to Blank's levels (my synthesis, not from a source):
- Retrieval: Levels 1 and 2.
- Vocabulary: Levels 2 and 3.
- Sequencing: Level 3.
- Prediction: Levels 3 and 4.
- Inference and explain: Level 4.

## 3. Year 2 national curriculum (England)

Source: English programmes of study, key stages 1 and 2 (DfE, September 2013). The Year 2 comprehension list is on page 18.

In plain words, Year 2 children should:
- Listen to and discuss poems, stories and information books that are harder than they can read alone.
- Discuss word meanings and link new words to words they already know. Talk about favourite words and phrases.
- Retell familiar tales and notice repeated language in stories.
- Talk about the order of events and how facts connect.
- Understand both the books they can read and the books read to them. That means using what they know, checking the text makes sense, and correcting their own reading when it does not.
- Make inferences from what characters say and do.
- Answer and ask questions about the text.
- Predict what happens next, based on what they have read so far.
- Take turns in discussion, listen to others, and explain their understanding clearly.
- Children who do not yet decode well should still follow the Year 2 comprehension programme, so they hear and talk about new books.

Currency: the 2013 document is the one I could read. A curriculum review is under way, with a new primary curriculum planned for delivery from 2028. That last point comes from a search summary, not the full article. Check gov.uk before quoting it to a school.

## 4. Multiple-choice advice for early readers

Evidence note: I found no research on multiple-choice design for six-year-olds in the sources I could open. Points marked "design" are my suggestions. The others rest on the sources listed.

- Read the question and options aloud (design, supported by the DfE wording). Year 2 expects children to understand texts they hear as well as texts they read. Reading the question aloud tests comprehension, not decoding. Keep a Read aloud button on every card.
- Pictures for Levels 1 and 2 (design). Use three clearly different pictures, the same size and style, so none stands out. Avoid near-twins such as a dog and a puppy.
- Words with a picture cue for Levels 3 and 4 (design). Keep option words to ones Finn can already decode, so reading load does not hide the comprehension. Keep each option to about eight words.
- Three options, correct answer in a random position (design). Avoid "all of these". The correct answer should not always be the longest or most detailed option.
- Gentle "try again" routine (design, drawing on the EEF's point about modelling inference by asking questions aloud):
  - Read the question aloud again.
  - Point to the picture or sentence that holds the clue.
  - Ask one question: "What do you notice here?"
  - Let Finn choose again.
  - After a second wrong choice, show the answer, read the clue sentence aloud, and say why in one sentence. No score, no buzzer, no red cross.
- No scores or buzzers (design, supported by Mueller and Dweck, 1998). In Study 3 of the paper, 38 per cent of children praised for ability misreported their scores to unfamiliar children. That compares with 13 per cent of children praised for effort and 14 per cent in the control group. Visible scores raise the stakes and invite fudging.

## 5. Praise and feedback language

What the evidence says:
- Praise for being clever (Mueller and Dweck, 1998): children praised for intelligence chose easier tasks, cared more about looking smart, and lost persistence and enjoyment after a setback. Children praised for effort chose harder tasks and kept going. Study 1 had 128 fifth graders aged 10 to 12. The paper also cites a kindergarten study (Kamins and Dweck, 1997) with a similar pattern. Most of the evidence is for older children, so treat it as a guide, not proof for six-year-olds.
- EEF guidance on feedback, as summarised in a Durrington Research School page: feedback should focus on the task, the process or self-regulation, not on personal attributes. I could not open that page or the EEF's own report (both returned HTTP 403). The wording comes from the search summary.
- Growth mindset effects are weak (Sisk et al., 2018). The correlation meta-analysis covered 273 studies and 365,915 students. The intervention meta-analysis covered 43 studies and 57,155 students. Both found weak overall effects. Some benefit was seen for students at risk or on low incomes. So praise words are a light touch, not a fix.

Phrases that work (short, about the task, no scores):
- "You looked back at the picture to check. Good idea."
- "You sounded that word out, and it worked."
- "You found the clue on that page."
- "That was a careful guess. How did you know?"
- "You kept going, even when it was tricky."
- "Not yet. Let's look again together."
- "You're getting better at finding clues."
- "Tell me how you worked that out."

Phrases to avoid:
- Person praise: "You're so clever", "You're a brilliant reader".
- Outcome-only praise: "Well done, you got it right!"
- Verdicts: "Wrong", "No", buzzers, scores.
- Comparisons with a sibling or other children.

For Dad: praise the thing Finn did, then ask how he worked it out. That matches the Year 2 expectation to explain understanding.
