# Facts for two stories: Triceratops and black holes

Research brief for a six-year-old reader. Written 11 October 2026.

How to read the tags:
- [checked] = I opened the page and saw the number or sentence.
- [search] = the fact came from a search snippet only, or the page could not be opened.
- (my comparison) = a picture I made to help a child. It is not from a source.

Numbers are kept as the source gives them. Where sources disagree, both are shown.

## Section A. Triceratops ("three-horned face")

### A1. When it lived and where its fossils are found

- Triceratops lived about 68 to 66 million years ago, in the Late Cretaceous, the last part of the dinosaur age. Comparison: long before any people lived (my comparison). Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/triceratops.html [checked]
- Its fossils are found in the USA. Source: same NHM page [checked].
- Many come from the Hell Creek rock layer. The UC Museum of Paleontology places it in north-eastern Montana, where a juvenile Triceratops skull was found. Source: https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/science/parks/hellcreek.php [checked]
- The Hell Creek rock layer covers parts of Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota and Wyoming. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hell_Creek_Formation [checked]. Wikipedia is not a museum, so quote the UCMP page for children.

### A2. How big it was

- It was about 9 metres long, about as long as a bus (my comparison). Source: NHM [checked] (9.0 m); McGill Redpath Museum, https://www.mcgill.ca/redpath/node/2815 [checked] (9 m, adult).
- It weighed about 10 tonnes (10,000 kg). Comparison: as heavy as about 500 six-year-olds (my comparison; a six-year-old weighs about 20 kg). Source: NHM [checked]. McGill gives a range of 6 to 12 tonnes [checked].
- Height is not given on the NHM or McGill pages [checked]. Sources disagree: about 3 m (https://simple.wikipedia.com/wiki/Triceratops) [search], or about 2 m at the hips (https://www.enchantedlearning.com/subjects/dinosaurs/facts/Triceratops/) [search]. Comparison for 2 m: about as tall as a grown-up's front door (my comparison). Story wording: "about two to three metres tall".

### A3. Its horns

- Its two long brow horns, one above each eye, could be about 1 metre long. Guinness says each could reach up to 1.2 m, and one named specimen had horns of 1.15 m each. Source: https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/93291-longest-dinosaur-horns [checked]. Comparison: about one and a half grown-up arms, shoulder to fingertip (my comparison; a grown-up arm is about 0.7 m).
- Guinness does not say whether these figures are the bone or the whole horn. The NHM says a horn covering made of keratin (the same material as fingernails) made the horns longer and sharper in life than the bone shows. Source: NHM [checked].
- It had a shorter horn on its nose. No length checked. Source: https://simple.wikipedia.com/wiki/Triceratops [search].

### A4. Its frill

- The frill could reach nearly one metre across. Comparison: about as wide as a small table (my comparison). Source: NHM [checked].

### A5. What the horns and frill were for

- The horns might have been used to fend off attacks from Tyrannosaurus. Source: NHM [checked].
- Males used their horns to fight each other, probably to impress females. Puncture marks on fossil frills show this. Source: NHM [checked].
- The frill might have protected the neck, and may have helped members of the same kind recognise each other. Source: NHM [checked].
- Did they lock horns? The evidence supports it. Farke, Wolff and Tanke (PLOS ONE, 28 January 2009) found a higher rate of injury marks (lesions) on the frill bone called the squamosal in Triceratops than in the related Centrosaurus (P = 0.002). They concluded the horns had a real role in physical combat. Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0004252 [checked]. The study shows marks, not whether they healed.
- The horns and frill changed shape as the animal grew, and may have helped it attract a mate. Source: https://www.mcgill.ca/redpath/node/2815 [checked].

### A6. Beak and teeth

- Triceratops was a plant eater. It ate tough palm fronds. Source: NHM [checked].
- It had a sharp, horny beak to snip and clip plants, and rows of shearing teeth at the back of its jaws. Source: NHM [checked]. Comparison: a beak like a parrot's (my comparison; no source I opened says parrot).
- It may have had between 144 and 160 teeth, which were replaced completely two to four times in its life. Comparison: about five times as many teeth as an adult person has (my comparison; adults have up to 32). Source: American Museum of Natural History, Fossil Friday, https://tumblr.amnh.org/post/806564151526866944/its-fossil-friday-with-its-massive-horns-and/amp [checked]
- Its teeth were packed in stacks called a dental battery, with only one working tooth in each column. The count is 15 to 35 columns. Source: https://archive.org/download/biostor-118503/biostor-118503.pdf [search]

### A7. Same time as Tyrannosaurus rex

- Tyrannosaurus rex also lived 68 to 66 million years ago, so the two lived at the same time. Source: NHM Tyrannosaurus page, https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/tyrannosaurus.html [checked]
- The UCMP page lists both as dinosaurs of the Hell Creek rock layer. Source: https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/science/parks/hellcreek.php [checked]
- Left out on purpose: the NHM page's bite-mark details (no gore).

### A8. A famous skeleton or skull?

- The NHM London Dino Directory page does not mention a Triceratops skeleton or skull on show. Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/triceratops.html [checked]
- The Smithsonian in Washington has a famous Triceratops mount called Hatcher, shown since 1905. It is built from bones of about ten animals, so it is a patchwork. Source: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/national-museum-of-natural-history/2018/08/10/elegy-hatcher-triceratops/ [search] (the page was blocked when opened). Also see https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/hatcher-the-first-triceratops-ever-put-on-display/sQERDarxJwtSnQ [search].
- A Triceratops skull found in Wyoming in 1891 belongs to the Smithsonian. It had been on loan to the University of Colorado Boulder since 1981, and a Smithsonian team was due to take it back to Washington in May 2022. Source: https://colorado.edu/today/2022/04/20/cu-boulders-beloved-triceratops-returning-home-smithsonian [checked]

### A9. Fossil hunting rules

Scotland:
- If you find an exceptional or unusual fossil, do not try to take it out. Seek advice from an expert. Source: Scottish Fossil Code, as reproduced by the Scottish Geology Trust, https://www.scottishgeologytrust.org/?p=7992 [checked]. NatureScot's own page returned an error when opened.
- Lawfully, you should get permission before taking fossils from land. Source: same page [checked].

England and Wales:
- No single national code was found. Local codes exist, such as West Dorset (Charmouth), where digging into undisturbed rock without the landowner's permission is not allowed. Source: https://charmouth.org/chcc/the-fossil-collecting-code/ [search]
- UK Fossils suggests contacting a local museum if a find might be important. Source: https://ukfossils.co.uk/code-of-conduct/ [search]

USA:
- On federal land you need a permit to collect a fossil. Source: 16 U.S.C. 470aaa-3(a)(1), https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/16/470aaa-3 [checked]
- On Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Reclamation and Forest Service land, the law says casual collecting must be allowed without a permit, if it fits the rules for that land. Source: same page, 470aaa-3(a)(2) [checked].
- The casual exception covers only common invertebrate and plant fossils, not backbone fossils such as dinosaur bones. Source: https://openei.org/wiki/RAPID/Roadmap/16_(3) [search]

## Section B. Black holes and space

### B1. Space is silent

- Sound needs atoms or molecules to carry it, and space has almost none, so sound cannot travel through it. Comparison: like shouting in a room with no air (my comparison). Source: https://coolcosmos.ipac.caltech.edu/ask/241--Can-you-hear-sound-in-space [checked]. This is an IPAC (Caltech) website, and its footer credits NASA contracts. It is not on nasa.gov.

### B2. Gravity and light

- A black hole's gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can get out past its edge. Comparison: light is the fastest thing there is (my comparison). Source: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/ [checked]
- The edge is called the event horizon. Source: same NASA page [checked].

### B3. Not a vacuum cleaner

- Black holes do not suck up everything. Far enough away, a black hole pulls with the same gravity as any other object of the same mass. Source: same NASA page [checked].
- Story idea: if the Sun were swapped for a black hole of the same mass, Earth would still feel the same pull and stay in its orbit (my comparison). Source for the idea: https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/educators/blackholes/imagine/page26.html [search]. This older NASA page dates from 1997 to 2004.

### B4. Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the centre of our galaxy

- Sagittarius A* sits at the centre of the Milky Way, about 27,000 light-years away. Comparison: its light has taken 27,000 years to reach us (my comparison; a light-year is the distance light travels in one year). Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]
- It has about four million times the mass of the Sun. Comparison: as heavy as four million Suns (my comparison). Source: ESO, same page [checked]
- Its first picture was released on 12 May 2022. Source: ESO, same page [checked]

### B5. How the picture was taken

- The Event Horizon Telescope linked eight radio observatories around the world. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/ [checked]
- It is a planet-scale array of eight ground-based radio telescopes, which makes a virtual Earth-sized telescope. Comparison: a telescope as wide as the whole Earth (my comparison). Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/ [checked]
- Nobody had to fly there, because the telescopes stayed on the ground (my comparison, from the word "ground-based"). Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/ [checked]

### B6. The first black hole picture (M87*)

- The first ever picture of a black hole, called M87*, was released on 10 April 2019. Source: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/ [checked]
- M87* is 55 million light-years away and has 6.5 billion times the mass of the Sun. Source: same ESO page [checked]
- Its event horizon measures just under 40 billion km across. The dark shadow it casts is about 2.5 times wider. Source: same ESO page [checked]
- Solar System comparison (my comparison): Pluto is on average 5.9 billion km from the Sun. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/dwarf-planets/pluto/facts/ [checked]. The Solar System out to Pluto is therefore about 12 billion km across. The event horizon is about three times that width.
- A scientist's quote that the image is larger than the whole Solar System appeared in a search snippet only. I have not used it.

### B7. The Milky Way spins

- The Sun, and so the Earth, travels around the centre of the Milky Way. One trip takes about 230 million years. Source: NASA StarChild, https://starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/StarChild/questions/question18.html [checked]. This page is from February 2000. Other estimates run from about 220 to 250 million years: https://www.livescience.com/how-long-galactic-year.html [search]
- The Sun moves at about 828,000 km/h. Comparison: about 7,000 times faster than a car on a motorway (my comparison; a motorway limit is about 113 km/h). Source: NASA StarChild, same page [checked]
- Comparison: one trip takes longer than the whole time the dinosaurs lived (my comparison, from my own knowledge, not checked).

### B8. Light from the Sun

- Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth. Comparison: about as long as a short TV cartoon (my comparison). Source: Smithsonian How Things Fly, https://howthingsfly.si.edu/ask-an-explainer/how-long-does-it-take-light-sun-get-earth [checked]
- The Sun is about 150 million km away. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/ [checked]. This NASA page gives the distance but not the minutes. StarChild gives 8.5 minutes [search].
