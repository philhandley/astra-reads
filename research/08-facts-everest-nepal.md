## 1. How tall Everest is

- Everest is 8,848.86 metres tall, a figure China and Nepal jointly announced on 8 December 2020. Source: https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2020/12/8/mt-everest-nepal-china-announce-revised-height-at-8848-metres [checked]
- The Al Jazeera headline rounds this to 8,849 m, but the article text gives 8,848.86 m. Source: same page [checked]
- National Geographic Explorer gives the height as 8,848 metres (29,029 feet). Source: https://explorer-mag.nationalgeographic.org/pathfinder_november_december_2019/five_facts_about_mount_everest [checked]
- Everest is the tallest mountain above sea level. Source: same NatGeo Explorer page [checked]
- Ben Nevis, Britain's highest mountain, is 1,345 metres high (Ordnance Survey, 2016). Source: https://www.countryfile.com/news/ben-nevis-britains-highest-mountain-grows-a-metre [checked]
- Everest is about six and a half Ben Nevises stacked on top of each other. (my comparison: 8,849 divided by 1,345 is about 6.6)
- More than 4,800 people have reached the top, some of them more than once. Source: NatGeo Explorer page [checked]
- NatGeo says the mountain grows about 6.4 mm a year. Other sites give 10 mm and 44 mm [search], so treat the rate as uncertain. Source: NatGeo Explorer page [checked]

## 2. Where it is and what it is called

- Everest straddles the border between Nepal and Tibet, which is part of China. Source: https://www.thebmc.co.uk/everest-facts-and-figures [checked]
- Everest is on the crest of the Great Himalayas, in southern Asia. Source: https://www.britannica.com/summary/Mount-Everest [search]
- Nepal calls the mountain Sagarmatha. NatGeo Explorer says this means "holy mother" [checked]; the BMC page says it means "brow of the sky" [checked]. The two sources disagree, so the story should avoid a single meaning.
- Tibetans call it Chomolungma (NatGeo spells it Chomolangma), which NatGeo says means "mother of the universe". Source: NatGeo Explorer page [checked]
- Britannica gives "Goddess Mother of the World" for Chomolungma [search]. Source: https://www.britannica.com/summary/Mount-Everest
- A local name was recorded on an 18th-century map of Tibet. Source: BMC page [checked]
- The English name honours George Everest, a British surveyor. Source: NatGeo Explorer page [checked]

## 3. The first people to reach the top

- Edmund Hillary, a New Zealander, and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa, were the first two people to reach the summit. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/adventure/features/everest/sir-edmund-hillary-tenzing-norgay-1953/ [checked]
- They reached the top on 29 May 1953, at 11:30 a.m. according to that article. Source: same NatGeo page [checked]
- That was over seventy years ago. (my comparison: 2026 minus 1953 is 73 years)
- The British team hired Tenzing as its lead Sherpa, or sirdar. Source: same NatGeo page [checked]

## 4. Thin air and the cold

- The air at the summit has about a third of the oxygen pressure of the air at sea level. Source: Tom Hornbein, NOVA Online, https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/everest/exposure/higher.html [checked]
- (my comparison: three breaths at sea level bring in about as much oxygen as one breath at the top)
- NatGeo Explorer says the summit has 66 per cent less oxygen than sea level. Source: NatGeo Explorer page [checked]
- Most climbers use bottled oxygen. From 1990 to 2006, more than 95 per cent of people who reached the summit used extra oxygen. Source: https://mounteverest.info/?p=5246 [checked] (this page cites PubMed for the figure)
- Summit temperatures are very cold. In midwinter the summit air is about minus 36 degrees Celsius. Source: International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 2021, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8000062/ [checked]
- In May, the climbing season, the average summit temperature is about minus 26 degrees Celsius. Source: same paper, from its table [checked]
- (my comparison: a home freezer is usually a little below minus 18 degrees Celsius, so the summit in May is colder than a freezer; the freezer figure is not from a source)

## 5. How long an expedition takes and the trek to base camp

- Most expeditions to Everest take around two months. Source: BMC page [checked]
- Climbers start arriving at the base camps in late March. Source: BMC page [checked]
- (my comparison: two months is longer than a school summer holiday)
- One north-side operator plans an 8-week pre-acclimatisation cycle before base camp, so the body can get used to the height. Source: https://alpenglowexpeditions.com/blog/let-the-countdown-to-everest-season-begin [checked]
- Everest Base Camp on the Nepal side is at 5,364 metres (17,701 feet). Source: https://nepalalternativetreks.com/everest-base-camp-trek-elevation-5364-meters-17701-feet/ [checked] (a trekking company page)
- (my comparison: base camp is about four times as high as Ben Nevis, since 5,364 divided by 1,345 is about 4.0)
- The trek often starts with a flight from Kathmandu to Lukla, which takes about half an hour. Source: same Nepal Alternative Treks page [checked]
- Lukla airport is at about 2,800 metres. Source: same page [checked] (my comparison: about twice as high as Ben Nevis)
- The base camp trek is often planned as 12 days. Source: same page [checked]. Another operator lists both 10-day and 12-day versions. Source: https://www.everesttrekkingroutes.com/activities/everest-base-camp-trek-12-days/ [checked]

## 6. Sherpa people and a record climber

- Sherpa is the name of a people, not a job title. Source: NBC News, https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna55013442 [checked] (an older article)
- Sherpas live mainly in the Solukhumbu region of eastern Nepal, in the shadow of Everest. Source: same NBC page [checked]
- Respectful fact: most Sherpas now see their work as mountaineering guides with considerable pride. Source: same NBC page [checked]
- Tenzing Norgay and Apa Sherpa are named as famous climbers, and Apa Sherpa climbed Everest 21 times. Source: same NBC page [checked]
- Britannica says there are about 150,000 Sherpas across Nepal, Sikkim in India, and Tibet. Source: https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sherpa-people [search]. NBC gives about 154,000 in an older article [checked].
- Kami Rita Sherpa has reached the top of Everest 32 times, the most of anyone, on 17 May 2026 at 10.12 a.m. local time. Source: Anadolu Agency, https://aa.com.tr/en/sports/nepali-mountaineer-kami-rita-sherpa-summits-everest-for-record-32nd-time/3939924 [checked]
- (my comparison: Finn is six, and 32 is more than five times his age)

## 7. Turnaround times

- Climbers aim to reach the summit in the morning, with plenty of daylight left to get down before nightfall. Source: BMC page [checked]
- Adrian Burgess, writing for the American Alpine Club in 1990, says teams should choose an early turn-around time, "such as two P.M.", and keep to it. Source: https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/12199023100/Asia-Nepal-Everest [checked]
- Not used: the 1996 season and the storm that followed it, which the brief asks us to avoid. The Burgess article's wording on accidents is also left out.

## 8. Trek features

- The Hillary Suspension Bridge is named after Edmund Hillary and hangs far above the Dudh Koshi River. Source: https://nepaltraveller.com/sidetrack/hillary-suspension-bridge [checked]
- It is the last obstacle before Namche Bazaar, and the trail then climbs about 2,500 feet to reach the town. Source: same Nepal Traveller page [checked]
- Yaks transport goods across mountain passes for local farmers and traders. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yak [checked]
- Yaks have larger lungs and hearts than cattle at lower altitudes. Source: same Wikipedia page [checked]
- Yaks can be hired at trailheads to carry your bags. Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/himalayas-hiking-practical-guide [checked]
- It is not unusual to see yaks resting near hikers on the trail. Source: same NatGeo page [checked]
- Prayer flags are believed to send prayers into the air as the wind strikes them. Source: https://www.markhorrell.com/blog/2012/prayer-flags-what-are-they/ [checked] (a travel blog post from 2012)
- Sources disagree on which colour matches which element on prayer flags. Source: https://www.mountainkingdoms.com/blog/the-meaning-of-prayer-flags [search] (the page returned 404 when opened)

## 9. Nepal's deep gorge and a famous waterfall

- The Kali Gandaki Gorge is in Nepal, between the peaks of Dhaulagiri and Annapurna. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kali_Gandaki_Gorge [search]
- The riverbed is about 2,520 metres high, which is 5,571 metres below the top of Annapurna I (8,091 m). Source: same Wikipedia page [checked]
- (my comparison: 5,571 metres is about four Ben Nevises stacked on top of each other)
- Wikipedia says that "by some sources" it may be one of the deepest gorges in the world. Source: same page [checked]
- A geology paper describes it as more than 6,000 metres deep. Source: https://virtualexplorer.com.au/system/files/papers/337/assets/deepest-canyon-earth.pdf [search]
- Devi's Fall, near Pokhara, is a waterfall where the river flows about 500 feet (roughly 150 metres) through a natural tunnel underground. Source: https://nepaltraveller.com/sidetrack/the-devis-falls-pokhara [checked]
- (my comparison: 150 metres is about one and a half football pitches)
- The local name is Patale Chango, which Nepal Traveller translates as "underground waterfall". Source: same page [checked]. The Pokhara municipality gives "Fall of the Netherworld" [search] for Patale Chhango, at https://tourism.pokharamun.gov.np/devis-fall.html.
- The water passes through the Gupteshwor Mahadev cave after the fall. Source: same Nepal Traveller page [checked]
- The page's account of how the fall got its English name involves a death, so the story should leave it out.

## 10. Crampons, ice axes and clip-on safety

- Crampons are spiked metal pieces worn on boots to help walk on ice and snow. Source: https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/crampon [search] (blocked when opened)
- (my comparison: like the cleats on football boots, but for ice)
- Climbers step onto their crampons and fit them to the boot. Source: https://www.alanarnette.com/2013/04/10/everest-2013-first-steps-into-the-icefall/ [checked] (Alan Arnette's 2013 blog)
- An ice axe is used for balance on the icefall. Source: same Arnette page [checked]
- An ice axe is also used to cut footholds and as an anchor point. Source: https://www.dictionary.com/browse/ice-axe [search] (Collins dictionary entry)
- Climbers stay clipped in to a safety rope while crossing the ladders in the icefall. Source: same Arnette page [checked]
- Some ropes are fixed in place by high-altitude workers. Source: BMC page [checked]
